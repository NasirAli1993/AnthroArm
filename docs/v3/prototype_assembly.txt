PHYSICAL PROTOTYPE PLAN — START WITH ONE FINGER
Project: D:\RoboticArm
Status: build plan and candidate hardware. No physical parts are connected.

WHAT THE DIGITAL MODEL MEANS
The v3 target is seven arm axes and twenty finger axes: 27 independently
commanded virtual joints. A virtual actuator is a mathematical torque source.
It does not select a real motor, gearbox, bearing, encoder or power supply.
The first bench prototype below has ONE physical servo. One tendon couples
three flexion hinges. It cannot reproduce independent control of the three
hinges or the finger's spread axis. The complete arm requires a separate
mechanical design and actuator-sizing review.

STARTER HARDWARE
Controller: Arduino UNO R3, powered from the PC over USB-B.
Servo candidate: Hitec D645MW, operated at a regulated 6.0 V for this plan.
Power: a separate enclosed, current-limited bench DC supply. A RIGOL DP711
is one documented example (0–30 V, 0–5 A), not a required purchase. Its maximum
output is not the servo setting. Set and meter the output at 6.0 V before use.
Camera: a USB webcam connected to the PC, optional for the first motor test.

The Hitec manufacturer's specification at 6.0 V gives 11.3 kgf·cm stall torque,
2.3 kgf·cm peak-efficiency torque, 0.4 A no-load running current and 2.15 A stall
current. Stall torque is NOT a usable continuous holding rating. The sheet
does not supply a continuous torque rating for this mechanism. Do not select
an arm motor by multiplying this stall number by an assumed safety factor.
The documented pulse range is 900–2100 microseconds, centered at 1500, with
default ±60 degree travel. The supplied commissioning firmware deliberately
uses only 1450–1550 microseconds until the actual mechanism is calibrated.

MECHANICAL STARTER ASSEMBLY
1. Use a firmly clamped base with a guarded, unloaded three-segment finger.
   The attractive full-hand shells are visual CAD. Confirm that the selected
   fabrication model contains actual pin bores, mating clearances, stops,
   tendon guides and mounting holes before printing or machining it.
2. Assemble the three flexion hinges by hand with smooth pins and retention.
   The separate printable_finger concept uses M2 reference pins in 2.3 mm
   nominal printed bores. Verify the actual printed fit, manufacturing
   clearance and retention. This is not a validated bearing fit.
3. Add one flexor tendon through the 1.4 mm guide channels on the palm
   side of the separate prototype. Verify the tendon fit. Fix its end
   at the distal segment. Use a light elastic return or a properly specified
   extension tendon. The return must open the finger without motor power.
4. Mount the servo to the base so tendon load does not bend its electrical
   cable or side-load an unsupported drum. A purchased 25-tooth horn matches
   the D645MW spline; a custom drum bolts to that horn. Do not assume a printed
   spline is strong or dimensionally accurate.
5. Choose the drum radius after measuring tendon travel. The proposed 7 mm radius
   winds about 14.7 mm of tendon over 120 degrees.
   This does not prove that the finger can close. For a 7 mm drum, 0.10 N·m
   corresponds to 14.3 N ideal tendon force before friction; it is an example,
   not an approved force setting. Keep fingers clear of the mechanism.
6. With motor power disconnected, move every hinge over its intended travel.
   Mark the mechanically safe open and closed tendon positions. Add hard
   stops before an overtravel can bind a joint or pull out a tendon anchor.
7. Leave the tendon detached for electrical commissioning. Connect it only
   after verifying the servo direction, neutral and conservative travel.

EXACT LOW-VOLTAGE CONNECTION NETS
See one_finger_wiring.svg for the same connections.

N1: PC USB port -> UNO R3 USB-B socket (data + board power).
N2: PC USB port -> USB camera (optional; data + camera power).
N3: UNO D9 -> servo signal pin S. This wire carries control pulses only.
N4: DC supply positive -> inline fuse F1 -> latching normally-closed motor
    stop S1 -> servo positive terminal. Supply set to 6.0 V.
N5: DC supply negative -> servo negative terminal, with a direct return wire.
N6: UNO GND -> the same supply-negative/servo-ground junction.

Do not join servo positive to UNO 5V, VIN, 3V3 or any I/O pin. The USB-powered
controller and dedicated servo supply share only their ground reference and
the signal connection. Verify the actual servo connector's S/+/- order using
the manufacturer's documentation and a meter; colors alone are insufficient.

F1 and S1 are functional requirements, not fully selected catalog parts.
Select F1 from the conductor/connector ampacity, startup pulse, supply fault
current and the fuse's time-current curve. S1 must interrupt the actual DC
motor load at the available voltage/current; a resistive AC rating is not an
equivalent motor-load rating. Use enclosed terminals with strain relief. Do
not carry servo power through a solderless breadboard or a thin signal lead.
Wire gauge and connector rating remain to be finalized from length, current,
temperature and allowable voltage drop. The manufacturer's 22 AWG servo lead
does not automatically qualify an entire harness of arbitrary length.

FIRMWARE BUILD STATUS
The UNO R3 firmware compiled successfully: 4,900 bytes of flash and 314 bytes
of RAM. Compilation verifies a build, not wiring, movement or hardware safety.
No physical board has been flashed or tested.

FIRST POWER AND FIRMWARE CHECK
1. Disconnect the tendon and support the servo mechanically. Latch S1 OFF.
2. With supply output OFF, check polarity, continuity and absence of a short
   between positive and ground. Verify F1, wiring and S1 ratings first.
3. Connect only USB to the UNO. Load the compiled single-finger firmware only
   after identifying the correct board and serial port. It starts DISARMED.
   No upload or physical test has occurred in this project without hardware.
4. Meter the isolated supply at 6.0 V. Use current limiting for the unloaded
   first test. A conservative starting ceiling of 0.5 A is a commissioning
   assumption; startup may current-limit. If that happens, power off and
   inspect the mechanism and connections before changing the limit. Do not
   repeatedly pulse a stalled or resetting servo.
5. Enable motor power with the tendon still detached, then ARM at 1500 us.
   Send small SET changes within 1450–1550 us while keeping the physical stop
   accessible. The bridge must send KEEPALIVE. DISARM detaches command pulses.
6. Confirm the 750 ms communication timeout and software DISARM behavior with
   an unloaded servo. A servo can keep holding or behave differently after
   losing pulses, so these software actions are not a motor-power stop.
7. Press S1 and verify that servo supply voltage is removed and motion stops.
   Releasing S1 can re-energize the servo; disarm software first, keep the
   mechanism unloaded and do not let reset alone trigger a motion command.
8. Power down, fit the tendon with minimal preload and repeat tiny movements.
   Log pulse, tendon travel, joint angles, current and temperature. Increase
   the envelope only after inspecting all hinge and tendon clearances.
9. Test an empty mechanism before low-force contact with a compliant object.
   Determine a safe current/travel limit from measurements. No payload or
   grip-force rating is established by the simulation's lift demonstration.

EXPANDING TO THE FULL HAND AND ARM
Use prototype_planning.xlsx: One finger BOM, Full arm plan and Actuator sizing.
The complete subsystem plan is included from the start, but drive selections
remain unresolved until loads are known. A practical order is:
  one unloaded finger -> instrumented finger -> fixed full hand -> wrist ->
  supported arm joints -> guarded full arm -> camera-guided object handling.

For each physical axis, measure or calculate downstream mass, center of mass,
payload lever arm, worst-case pose, desired speed/acceleration, friction,
transmission ratio/efficiency, duty cycle and thermal environment. Calculate
joint torque using gravity + inertia + external/contact load, then validate
continuous and peak torque against a manufacturer's performance data.
The worksheet contains editable inputs and preliminary arithmetic; a blank
input is not treated as zero. It does not perform multibody dynamics, finite
element analysis, gearbox selection or machine safety certification.

Seven arm drives may require gear reduction, joint bearings independent of
the motor shaft, absolute encoders, holding brakes or mechanical support,
hard stops, cable routing and a power distribution system. Twenty virtual
hand axes might use direct drives, remote tendons or coupled mechanisms;
their physical motor count depends on that design. Do not buy 27 hobby servos
and assume they can reproduce the virtual model's effort limits.

For comparison only, ROBOTIS publishes an XH540-W270 smart actuator's stall
point of 9.9 N·m at 12 V and 4.9 A. It is not selected for this arm. Stall is
not continuous torque; compare the relevant performance curve and thermal
duty against each joint's calculated demand. Large shoulder or elbow loads
may exceed this actuator's usable capability. Power loss on a gravity-loaded
arm can cause falling links; provide suitable holding/support provisions.

CAMERA IMITATION AND AUTONOMY ARE DIFFERENT
Imitation: webcam -> hand landmarks -> joint targets. A person's hand supplies
the desired pose. It is teleoperation, not independent object selection.
Autonomy: calibrated camera -> object detection -> depth/3D localization ->
camera-to-robot transform -> reachable target -> collision-aware plan ->
controller -> sensors verify outcome. A plain webcam does not directly give
metric depth. A depth camera or calibrated multi-view method needs separate
calibration and validation. The v3 virtual RGB-D experiment uses rendered
sensor data; it does not demonstrate real-camera grasp reliability.
Use fresh frame timestamps, bounded workspace, confidence checks and physical
stop controls before connecting vision output to a physical actuator.
